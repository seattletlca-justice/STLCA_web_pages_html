A month before the eviction, the City already had notice. The eviction happened anyway, and the complaints it generated were closed the way most of this property’s complaints were closed — without an inspection.

On July 20, 2020, Seattle Police Chief Carmen Best signed a Notice of Declaration of Chronic Nuisance Property for 8201 Aurora Ave N, addressed to the registered owner, Next Wave Investments LLC, attention Myung Ha Kang. The letter lists 25 documented incidents at the property over the preceding twelve months, each with its own SPD incident number: five reported rapes, seven assaults, drug activity, promoting prostitution, and one homicide on May 26, 2020.

What the ordinance actually does — SMC 10.09A chronic nuisance declaration triggers a Correction Agreement between the property owner and police, with penalties of up to $500 a day against the person in charge and up to $25,000 against the owner for non-cooperation. The statute governs the owner’s obligation to abate the nuisance. It does not authorize, require, or mention evicting tenants.

Five days after the owner posted notices telling residents they had 24 hours to leave, SPD signed that Correction Agreement — after the evictions had already started, not before them. An attorney quoted by PubliCola at the time called the sequence “incredibly problematic.”

Fourteen tenants filed complaints with SDCI describing what was happening to them in real time, during an active COVID-19 eviction moratorium:

“Mass eviction taking place landlord has shut off water, hired security to stop tenants form entering, boarding up windows – everyone need to be out in 24 hrs.”007155-20CP, filed 08/18/2020

Every one of the fourteen was closed “Admin Closure.” None has a captured inspection in the record. The inspector of record on several of them, per the same-day status changes in SDCI’s own timeline, was Stella Washington.

What follows is argument, not record: the City had its own documented notice — SPD’s nuisance declaration — a full month before the eviction began. SDCI had fourteen of its own complaint records describing the eviction as it happened. Nothing in the record shows either agency intervening to stop it while it was still happening. The full argument, and its limits, belong to a reader who has already seen the fourteen records below — not to a summary above them.

Two years earlier, in 2018, Everspring Inn had been the single largest recipient of Rapid Rehousing funds paid to any landlord or vendor in the city that year — at least $164,000, city-funded money meant to move homeless residents indoors.

The habitability enforcement came a year after the eviction, not during it. A Notice of Violation issued July 27, 2021 cites 148 separate code violations — not the 113 an earlier pass at this same document undercounted, a parsing gap corrected the same day this page was built. Compliance was claimed five days before the deadline, a status entry in SDCI’s portal, not a separately documented re-inspection. The elevator’s certificate shows an unresolved $663.68 balance as recently as the 2025 notice.
